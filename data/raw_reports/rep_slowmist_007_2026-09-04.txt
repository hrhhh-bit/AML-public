Recently, the CEO of OKX responded to a user post about a situation where a “gambling platform directly transferred funds to an exchange, causing the deposit to trigger a review.” According to his public statement, deposits to OKX from high-risk addresses may trigger stricter anti-money laundering (AML) and risk reviews. Depending on the specific circumstances, the review may last 15 days or even longer, during which certain account functions and funds may be restricted. For accounts confirmed to be involved in high-risk or illegal activities, the platform may also terminate its services.
OKX also reminded users that funds obtained through channels such as guaranteed transactions in Telegram groups, Huiwang, and its variants may carry a higher risk in terms of their source of funds.
The signal conveyed by this response is clear: exchanges are no longer concerned only with “who the user is,” but also with “where the money comes from and what it has been through.”
For exchanges, payment institutions, OTC brokers, custodians, and other crypto asset service providers, risk identification is moving from the account level to before funds enter the business system. In other words, a user completing KYC does not mean that every fund entering their account is inherently trustworthy; an address not directly matching a blacklist does not mean that it has no risk exposure.
Gambling platforms are not necessarily synonymous with illicit funds, but the gambling business has long been regarded by anti-money laundering authorities as a high-priority risk area. As early as 2008, FATF published risk-based approach guidance for casinos, explicitly including casinos and online casinos within the scope of relevant risk discussions. FATF’s subsequent research on the casino and gambling industry has also pointed out issues such as its cash-intensive nature, insufficient regulatory coverage, weak management of VIPs and intermediaries, and inadequate risk identification in online gambling.
The United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) has also pointed out that casinos, gambling intermediaries, and cryptocurrencies in East and Southeast Asia have become important components of underground banking, money laundering, and cyber fraud infrastructure. A typical money laundering method is to create a “legitimate appearance” for the source of funds through gambling transactions: illicit funds first enter a gambling account, then a record is created through small bets, internal transfers, or other transaction activities, after which the funds are withdrawn in the form of so-called gambling winnings.

In the past, AML/KYT work often started with blacklist screening: checking whether a target address matched sanctions, stolen funds, darknet, mixers, or other known high-risk labels. Such screening remains necessary, but with the rapid development of stablecoins, non-custodial wallets, P2P, OTC, cross-chain bridges, and DEXs, relying solely on a single address or direct transaction relationships has become insufficient to fully explain fund risks.
In actual risk control scenarios, checking only the final deposit address may fail to reveal the original source of risk; tracking only one-hop direct transactions may also make it impossible to identify the propagation of risky funds across multiple intermediate nodes. Conversely, if amount, proportion, and path attenuation are not taken into account, simply classifying an address as high risk because it has previously interacted with risky funds can easily lead to false positives and the wrongful flagging of legitimate users.
Therefore, the actual risk control process needs to answer at least four questions:
1. Does the address itself pose a risk?
2. Is there direct or indirect risk exposure?
3. How did the risky funds flow in?
4. Is the risk still ongoing?
