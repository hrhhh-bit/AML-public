In September 2026, the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) published the report Risks of Gaming and Gambling, providing a systematic analysis of money laundering, terrorist financing, and proliferation financing risks in areas including casinos, sports betting, other forms of gambling, and online gaming.
The value of this report lies not in treating all gaming and gambling activities as inherently high-risk, but in highlighting a development that deserves greater attention from virtual asset service providers: as online operations, cross-border activities, multiple products, and multiple payment methods converge, gaming and gambling platforms are becoming part of a broader value transfer ecosystem. For exchanges, VASPs, and virtual asset service providers offering services such as crypto payments, funds originating from this ecosystem should not be understood merely as a “gambling transaction,” but should instead be examined within the context of the broader flow of funds and financial relationships.
In the report, FATF identifies a wide range of specific risk indicators covering multiple dimensions, including customer and account behavior, betting patterns, payment and transaction activities, as well as platform and business models. This article will focus on selected indicators that are directly relevant to fund flows and on-chain risk identification. For the complete risk indicator framework and specific cases, readers may also refer to the original FATF report.
The traditional understanding of how gambling is used for money laundering is that funds enter a platform, pass through a small amount of or seemingly normal gambling activity, and are then withdrawn in another form. The Red Flags identified by FATF this time focus on such fund patterns that are inconsistent with legitimate business purposes.
For example, large amounts of funds are deposited and then quickly withdrawn with little or no gambling activity; multiple small amounts from different sources are consolidated into larger amounts before being withdrawn; funds are rapidly transferred to different accounts or beneficiaries; or an account uses multiple payment methods, or is funded by third parties whose relationship with the account holder is unclear.
These signals do not prove that a customer is engaged in illegal activity. Instead, they raise a more specific question: Is the platform being used for legitimate gambling, or is it being exploited to facilitate the entry, conversion, and exit of funds?
A sum of money entering a platform, being used for a small amount of betting, and then being withdrawn to another account may not appear unusual when each step is viewed in isolation. However, when deposits, betting, withdrawals, account relationships, and payment channels are considered together, the meaning of the transactions may change.
FATF points out that the gaming and gambling industry is becoming connected to various means of value transfer, including cash, bank cards, bank transfers, virtual assets, mobile payments, and third-party intermediaries. The shift toward online and cross-border operations has further reduced the geographical constraints on fund flows, while also making the relationships between different platforms, accounts, and services more complex.
Of particular note, FATF explicitly lists virtual assets as one of the payment methods used by the gaming and gambling industry, and points out that some payment methods, including virtual assets, may support rapid, anonymous, and cross-border transfers of value.
This means that risks do not necessarily remain within gambling platforms. When funds enter or leave the gambling ecosystem through virtual assets, the associated risks may further emerge at exchanges, payment institutions, and other virtual asset service platforms.
However, for virtual asset service providers, the fact that a customer address has transacted with a gambling platform, or has matched a gambling-related risk label, does not mean that the funds necessarily involve illegal activity. FATF also emphasizes that a single risk indicator alone cannot demonstrate the existence of money laundering, terrorist financing, or proliferation financing activity.
What really needs to be examined is what this connection means within the broader context of the financial relationship. For example, an address may have an indirect connection with a gambling entity, but assessing its risk also requires considering factors such as the number of hops, the amount and proportion of funds, transaction frequency, customer background, and historical behavior. Conversely, overlooking fund connections with high-risk or unlicensed platforms may also result in the omission of risk signals that warrant further investigation.
This approach has some common ground with our previous analysis of OKX’s risk control practices: when dealing with high-risk funds, exchanges need to look beyond whether an individual transaction matches a particular risk label and also consider information such as the source of funds, counterparties, direct or indirect exposure, and fund flows.

Therefore, for risks that span multiple platforms and business scenarios, a more valuable analytical approach is:
Who is the customer → Where do the funds come from → Which entities have they interacted with → Which addresses have the funds passed through → Are there persistent abnormal behaviors → Is the current transaction consistent with historical activity?
By combining these pieces of information, it is possible to move beyond a single risk label to reconstruct the underlying financial relationships and establish a more explainable and verifiable basis for risk assessment.
In practice, screening, investigation, and ongoing monitoring need to be connected with one another.
Institutions can first use address and transaction screening to identify known risk entities or risk types, and then conduct further investigations based on customer information, sources of funds, on-chain transaction counterparties, and fund flows. Where supported by evidence and applicable rules, they can then determine whether enhanced due diligence (EDD), restrictive measures, or reporting is required.
For businesses subject to ongoing monitoring, attention should be paid to new high-risk counterparties, abnormal fund aggregation, rapid dispersal and transfers, and transaction patterns that are inconsistent with a customer’s established behavior.
This is not the only process prescribed by FATF for all institutions, but rather a risk-based practical approach. The core principle is to ensure that every escalation and action taken can explain: what signal triggered the escalation, what information was verified, what kind of fund relationship was identified, and why the corresponding measures were taken.
This can help reduce misjudgments caused by relying solely on a single risk label, while also facilitating internal review, audits, and subsequent compliance record-keeping.
On-chain analysis tools cannot make final compliance determinations on behalf of institutions, but they can provide structured evidence to support the processes described above.
