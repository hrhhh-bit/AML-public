On September 18, Japan’s National Police Agency, together with the National Cybersecurity Office, the U.S. Federal Bureau of Investigation, the U.S. Department of Defense Cyber Crime Center, the Australian Signals Directorate’s Australian Cyber Security Centre, Germany’s Federal Intelligence Service, and the Federal Office for the Protection of the Constitution, released a joint report focusing on a North Korea-backed hacker group — WaterPlum (also known as “Contagious Interview”).
The Contagious Interview campaign has been tracked by multiple international security vendors for years and is often considered to be associated with North Korea’s Lazarus Group. This marks the first time that Japanese police have officially disclosed specific domestic cases and seized evidence in collaboration with multiple countries.
Report link: https://www.npa.go.jp/bureau/cyber/pdf/20260918_j.pdf
The core of WaterPlum’s approach is actually not complicated: impersonating recruiters.
Step 1: Bait Distribution
WaterPlum proactively contacts IT professionals through social media, job sites, gig platforms, and freelance marketplaces, with a particular focus on Web developers and engineers in the cryptocurrency/blockchain space. The impersonated identities may be AI companies, crypto asset companies, NFT project teams, or recruiters.
Step 2: The Technical Interview Trap
After making contact with a target, the typical approach is as follows: they invite you to participate in a “technical interview” or give you a programming test. During the test, they ask you to download a “project file” from a code repository such as GitHub, usually claiming that you need to “fix a bug in this video conferencing tool” or “complete the test according to this template.”
This file is often the malware itself.
Step 3: Malware Deployment
One notable example is a malware strain called StoatWaffle, which disguises itself as a blockchain-related project repository and uses a VS Code configuration file called .vscode/tasks.json to automatically execute code. In other words, simply opening the project folder and selecting “Trust” may cause the code to start running in the background, without requiring you to manually click Run.
The police also specifically warned that unfamiliar VS Code projects should always be opened in “Restricted Mode.” When the prompt “Do you trust the authors of the files in this folder?” appears, selecting “No” can help avoid this trap. At the same time, do not place unfamiliar projects into folders that you have previously marked as “trusted,” and do not disable the workspace trust security setting just for convenience.
Step 4: Data Theft and Lateral Movement
These malware strains are hidden inside NPM packages disguised as legitimate code repositories. Once executed, they install backdoors on the computer, connect to remote servers, and begin harvesting various types of sensitive information: account passwords stored in browsers, clipboard contents, keystrokes, screenshots, private keys and seed phrases from crypto wallets, as well as photos of identity documents such as driver’s licenses and passports.
The stolen account credentials are not simply sold for money. The police pointed out that attackers use these stolen credentials to gain access to the internal networks of the victims’ organizations and conduct deeper intrusions, such as stealing intellectual property and carrying out further lateral movement within corporate environments. The stolen identity document photos may also be used by North Korean IT workers to impersonate identities and enter overseas job markets to earn extra income.
From December 2025 to July 2026, the data obtained by the police showed that at least 30,000 computers across more than 100 countries and regions were infected, information from more than 7,000 crypto wallets was stolen, and assets worth at least approximately JPY 1.7 billion (around USD 10.71 million) flowed into wallets controlled by WaterPlum.
The most noteworthy part of this report is actually not the malware itself, but the fact that the police, for the first time, seized and dismantled a “Laptop Farm” in Japan.
Simply put, the North Korean IT workers were not physically in Japan, but they persuaded (or paid) someone living in Japan to place a computer in their home and then remotely control that computer to do the work. From the outside, it appeared that the job had been taken by a “local Japanese” person, while the actual operator might have been located in North Korea, China, or Russia.
The collaborator in Japan (referred to as a “facilitator” in the report) provided a place to keep the computer, internet access, and even their own identity documents for North Korean workers to use to impersonate them when taking jobs. In some cases, jobs taken by the facilitators were completed by North Korean workers “under their identity”; in others, the facilitators’ own bank accounts were designated as the receiving accounts, and after the money was transferred in, they would find ways to move it out. In addition, they also used VPS servers rented by the facilitators to further conceal the actual location where the work was being performed.
The police determined that through these methods, North Korean IT workers had transferred cryptocurrency and other assets worth hundreds of millions of yen overseas, and the report explicitly stated where the money was going: to uses such as weapons development. At the same time, the report also explicitly warned that outsourcing work to and paying compensation to North Korean IT workers may violate domestic laws and sanctions against North Korea.
In May 2025, Japanese cryptocurrency exchange bitFlyer received a job application for an engineering position. The application was later determined to have most likely come from a North Korean IT worker. The entire process was documented in this report, making it a particularly vivid case.
The applicant did not use any intermediary and submitted their résumé directly through the company’s official website. The contact email was a Gmail address, and a VPN was used when logging into the recruitment system (NETNUT Proxy, Astrill VPN, and High Speed Rabbit Proxy were all specifically mentioned). The skills listed on the résumé were quite “comprehensive”: the applicant claimed proficiency in more than a dozen programming languages, as well as expertise in blockchain and cloud services. The applicant also claimed to have graduated from a university in Europe and subsequently worked in several different countries.
bitFlyer was cautious and arranged a video interview. Looking back, a series of details during the interview now appear quite typical: the applicant looked Asian but claimed to be of Malaysian descent and to have lived in Finland for many years, with Malay and Chinese as their native languages. The entire interview was conducted in English, but the applicant’s English proficiency clearly did not match the education and work history listed on the résumé. When asked about specific skills listed on the résumé, the applicant could answer simple questions, but when the questions became slightly more in-depth, they began to evade them and gave very vague answers.
There were also other suspicious details: whenever relocating to Japan for work was mentioned, the applicant repeatedly made excuses or said, “Let’s see in six months”; they insisted on being paid in cryptocurrency; during the interview, they repeatedly glanced at another screen to the side, as if reading answers; other people could occasionally be heard speaking in the background; and the video feed frequently froze and dropped frames.
bitFlyer ultimately determined that something was wrong and did not hire the applicant. bitFlyer was also included in the acknowledgments section of this report.
The report states a clear assessment by Japan’s National Police Agency and the FBI: both WaterPlum’s cyberattack activities and the money-making activities of certain North Korean IT workers are directed by General Bureau 313 of the Munitions Industry Department of the Central Committee of the Workers’ Party of Korea.
A key piece of evidence supporting this assessment is the overlap in IP addresses: the IP addresses used by WaterPlum attackers, those used by North Korean IT workers to connect to laptop farms and crowdsourcing platforms, and those used by the North Korean worker who applied to bitFlyer when logging into the recruitment system were found to match in several instances.
The report also revealed some details about the day-to-day working conditions of WaterPlum members. For example, they use an AI face-swapping application called “Magicam” to get through video interviews, but generally only dare to use it for a few minutes before making an excuse such as “the network is bad” and turning off their cameras. On certain North Korean commemorative days, they are given special permission to play games for a period of time, mainly simulation and management games, and some have also been seen watching videos of football matches. In their daily work, they also use voice-reading software such as “NaturalReader” to practice Japanese pronunciation, while translation and AI tools are generally used in their free versions.

This report is not simply another disclosure of a malicious group with ties to North Korea. Instead, it demonstrates an increasingly complex attack pattern: attackers can enter personal devices through job postings, move from personal devices into corporate networks, and then monetize stolen credentials and crypto wallet assets directly. At the same time, another set of identity impersonation techniques and Laptop Farm infrastructure can help related individuals enter overseas IT labor markets. On one side, they steal crypto assets through phishing; on the other, they use fake identities to enter overseas labor markets. The two tracks are intertwined and provide cover for each other.
For individuals and businesses, security awareness should not focus solely on “preventing malware.” Recruitment, outsourcing, and identity verification should also be incorporated into security considerations. The report concludes by emphasizing that attack methods will continue to evolve and become more complex. Organizations and individuals should continue to monitor alerts from domestic and international security agencies, as well as reports published by security vendors.
